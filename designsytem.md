# Lakefront Digital Visual Research and Design-System Direction
## Executive summary
The strongest direction is to **keep the visual personality of the top-left 
concept, but make it more disciplined and more explicitly “digital services” 
than “surf brand.”** The selected reference already has the right ingredients: 
an illustrated breaking-water crest, deep navy structure, layered teal/aqua, 
white foam, a compact badge silhouette, and enough hand-crafted character to be 
memorable. The other two supplied studies are useful as reduction tests, but 
they lose too much of that personality when treated as the primary identity. 
That distinction matters because Lakefront Digital is not fundamentally a 
lifestyle or outdoor company. Your existing positioning is web design, 
WordPress customization and management, hosting, application deployment, 
performance, analytics, SEO, and e-commerce for small businesses. 
fileciteturn0file1 The identity therefore needs to carry **waterfront energy 
+ technical competence** rather than simply “ocean.” My recommendation is a 
visual system built around: **Deep Lake Navy → dark teal → turquoise → pale 
foam**, with **coral as the preferred fifth color**. Coral gives the system the 
warm counterpoint you were asking for without changing the aquatic identity. 
Mustard/gold is a credible alternative when you want a more established/premium 
feel, while seafoam is harmonious but provides less differentiation. Similar 
teal-plus-warm-accent combinations appear effectively in coastal identity work 
using sunset orange and in a deep-teal/gold wave identity. 
citeturn15search0turn15search9 The photography should **support**, not 
imitate, the logo. Use real water photography for atmosphere—wave detail, Lake 
Michigan/lake surfaces, aerial shore geometry, and credible waterfront-device 
imagery—while reserving the illustrated crest for the actual identity. The best 
logo architecture is not one logo but a **responsive family**: **illustrated 
crest badge → simplified G/wave mark → monochrome icon → wordmark → app/favicon 
mark**. That gives you the personality of the top-left study at large sizes 
without forcing fine foam detail into a 16 px favicon. Negative-space circular 
wave marks and wave-initial concepts are good evidence for how this can 
collapse gracefully to small formats.

## Brand visual thesis
The top-left reference has four qualities worth preserving. First, it has 
**movement**. The crest curves around itself, creating a visual loop rather 
than a static lake horizon. That is useful for a digital-services business 
because the mark can imply flow, deployment, iteration, traffic, data movement, 
and forward momentum without literally drawing servers or code. Second, it has 
**depth**. The dark-to-light teal bands make the wave feel substantial. That 
offers a much richer foundation for a design system than a single flat 
corporate blue. Third, it has **an ownable silhouette**. At a glance, the wave 
almost becomes a `G`, giving Lakefront Digital an opportunity to turn the 
negative space into a deliberate monogram instead of relying on a generic wave 
illustration. Fourth, it has **human texture**. The slight illustrative quality 
feels warmer than the very sterile SaaS/agency visual language that dominates 
blue-and-white technology branding. The main risk is category confusion. Large 
breaking waves, tropical turquoise, surf photography, palm imagery, script 
type, and distressed badges can quickly make the company resemble a surf shop, 
marina, beach resort, or environmental organization. Contemporary surf-oriented 
web concepts often combine cinematic waves, deep blues, adventure messaging, or 
coastal lifestyle cues; those are useful compositional references but should 
not define Lakefront Digital’s category. citeturn17search6turn17search11 
The correction is to pair the organic wave with **highly systematic digital 
components**: clean grids, exact spacing, restrained typography, geometric 
service icons, performance/data visualizations, browser/device frames, and 
structured cards. A recent teal-led agency concept is especially relevant 
because it treats teal as the dominant design language while retaining 
geometric iconography and systematic layouts; another “Wave” studio explicitly 
positions design and development together, showing that fluid visual language 
can coexist with technical services. citeturn17search5turn17search0 The 
design-system principle should therefore be:
> **Water supplies the motion. Engineering supplies the structure.**
That maps naturally to Lakefront Digital's documented combination of design, 
WordPress, hosting, performance, management, e-commerce, and deployment.

## Color system and design tokens
The photo research reinforces rather than changes the color direction. One of 
the selected turquoise texture photographs exposes colors around `#075459`, 
`#278891`, `#73AFBC`, and `#C1DCE4`, making those a particularly defensible 
foundation because they occur naturally together in the water imagery rather 
than being arbitrary UI colors. citeturn14search2

### Additional-color options
| Accent | Hex | Character | Best use | Assessment | ---|---:|---|---|---| 
| **Sunset Coral** | `#F26B5B` | Energetic, human, contemporary | Primary CTA, 
| active states, tiny logo highlight, statistics, link emphasis | 
| **Recommended** | **Harbor Gold** | `#D5A33A` | Established, crafted, premium 
| | Case-study highlights, premium services, awards, restrained CTA | Strong 
| alternative | **Seafoam Green** | `#79B89C` | Calm, ecological, approachable 
| | Background chips, success state, secondary illustration | Harmonious but 
| least differentiated |
**Coral is the best fit.** The brand already has ample cool color. Another 
aqua/green creates more harmony but does not solve the original problem of the 
identity feeling too blue/grey/white. Coral introduces temperature contrast 
while retaining the “sunset over water” logic. A coastal branding project using 
teal with sunset orange demonstrates the same broad relationship, while a 
deep-teal/gold wave identity shows why gold is the more premium alternative. 
citeturn15search0turn15search9 Coral should be an **accent, not a second 
dominant brand color**. In the palette above, Lake Navy against Coral 
calculates to approximately **5.01:1** contrast, whereas white against Coral is 
only about **2.99:1**. For normal-size interface text, WCAG 2.2 calls for at 
least 4.5:1, while large text has a 3:1 threshold. 
citeturn28search0turn28search1 Thus the clean button pattern is: `coral 
background + Lake Navy label` rather than: `coral background + white small 
text`. Gold and seafoam also work better with navy lettering than white.
### Palette modes
The system can support three useful modes without changing the identity: **Lake 
mode** uses Navy + Deep Water + Current Teal + Aqua + Coral. This should be the 
default website and brand mode. **Night-water mode** uses Navy as the canvas, 
off-white typography, aqua diagrams and coral controls. This is especially good 
for hosting, performance, security, infrastructure, and technical case-study 
sections. **Foam mode** uses off-white as the canvas, navy typography, subtle 
mist borders and isolated teal/coral highlights. This prevents the site from 
becoming an uninterrupted wall of dark blue.

### Recommended logo family
I would develop **three directions in parallel**, then test them instead of 
committing directly to one detailed logo. **Route A — Crest G** should be the 
lead. Keep the expressive curl of the top-left image, but make the negative 
space explicitly read as a `G`. Use three water layers: Navy/Deep Water, Teal, 
and Aqua; use white only for foam. Coral may appear as a *tiny* sun, spark, 
terminal, or registration detail, but should not turn the wave orange. **Route 
B — Lakefront horizon G** should be the more restrained alternative. Replace 
the dramatic ocean breaker with a freshwater curl/horizon combination inside a 
circle. This reduces the “surf company” risk while retaining the name's 
geographic metaphor. **Route C — Technical current** should be the 
digital-first option. Build a G or L from two or three flowing bands or 
data-like paths. It would be less illustrative than your selected reference but 
substantially more distinctive than the second supplied app-icon study. The 
current rounded-square concept you supplied is useful as a **stress test**, not 
as the finished primary mark. The vertical black bars plus blue water are 
readable, but they introduce a second metaphor that could be interpreted as a 
gate, equalizer, comb, server rack, or bridge. Unless that architecture has a 
deliberate story, the wave itself is the stronger asset. Likewise, the simple 
`Lakefront / Digital` typographic study is a useful restraint benchmark, but by 
itself it discards the most ownable part of the direction. I would use that 
minimalism in the **wordmark typography**, not remove the crest. The responsive 
architecture should look like this: ```mermaid flowchart LR
    A["Illustrated Crest + Lakefront Digital"] --> B["Simplified Crest + 
    Wordmark"] B --> C["G / Wave Symbol"] C --> D["One-color Micro Mark"] D --> 
    E["16–32 px Favicon"] C --> F["App / WordPress Icon"] B --> G["Proposal / 
    Site Header"] A --> H["Signage / Social / Campaign"]
``` The key test is not whether the full badge looks beautiful at 1000 px. It 
is whether the **same underlying geometry survives from a proposal cover down 
to a 16 px browser tab**.
## Web imagery, hero layouts, and typography
The website should not simply place a giant wave photograph behind white text. 
That would produce a visually strong homepage but make the photography and logo 
compete for the same role. A better hierarchy is: **logo = symbolic water** 
**photography = environmental water** **UI = technical structure** **coral = 
action**
### Primary hero recommendation
Use a **split or asymmetric hero**. The left 42–48% should be Lake Navy or Foam 
White with the value proposition, one short supporting paragraph, coral primary 
CTA, and a low-emphasis secondary action. The right 52–58% should use an aerial 
shoreline or broad wave crop. Aerial imagery is particularly effective here 
because it reads partly as landscape and partly as abstract geometry; the 
Ocealife coastal hero reference similarly uses a full-width aerial water 
photograph to create immersion. citeturn17search8 For Lakefront Digital I 
would keep more structure than that tourism example: straight type, clean grid, 
and no decorative script. A plausible first-screen composition: ```text 
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ [crest] 
LAKEFRONT DIGITAL Work Services │ │ │ │ Websites that perform.  
┌──────────────────────────┐ │ │ WordPress, hosting and digital │ │ │ │ systems 
built for small business.  │ aerial teal shoreline │ │ │ │ / foam │ │ │ [ Start 
a project ] View services │ │ │ │ └──────────────────────────┘ │ │ WordPress • 
Hosting • Ecommerce • Performance │ 
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ ``` This 
structure also aligns with your documented offer rather than forcing visitors 
to interpret a vague “creative agency” message. fileciteturn0file1
### Alternative dark hero
The second variant should use a full Lake Navy background with a **large 
simplified wave/G graphic behind the copy at very low visual intensity**, then 
place a smaller rectangular waterfront-tech photograph or browser mockup on the 
right. That is particularly appropriate when the headline emphasizes:
> **Design, hosting, and WordPress that move your business forward.**
The graphic wave supplies emotional identity; the browser/UI object confirms 
that this is a digital company. A teal-dominant agency reference demonstrates 
how a strong signature color, oversized brand typography, systematic geometric 
icons and agency content can coexist without relying on conventional black/red 
“creative agency” styling. citeturn17search5 Another agency landing-page 
example uses minimalist structure, bold typography, whitespace and restrained 
blue accents to communicate services and credibility. citeturn17search12
### Crop rules
Hero photography should be selected with responsive cropping in mind. For 
desktop, keep the wave crest, person, laptop, or shoreline intersection in the 
**right-center third**, leaving uncluttered water or sky toward the text side. 
For mobile, use a deliberately authored portrait crop instead of allowing 
`object-fit: cover` to arbitrarily remove the focal point. Water textures can 
tolerate aggressive cropping. Human lifestyle photographs cannot; faces, hands, 
laptops and the water relationship are semantically important. For text placed 
directly over an image, use a navy overlay or gradient and **measure the final 
rendered contrast**, rather than assuming that a dark photograph is sufficient. 
WCAG 2.2 specifies 4.5:1 minimum contrast for normal text and 3:1 for large 
text. citeturn28search0turn28search1
### Typography
My preferred pairing is: **Manrope 600–800 — headings, navigation, buttons** 
**Source Sans 3 400–600 — body, forms, documentation, long-form material** 
Manrope is an open-source modern sans-serif family, while Source Sans 3 
provides a broad, practical text family that is already used institutionally 
for body and communications work. citeturn18search1turn18search10 Both fit 
the design direction without competing with the illustrated mark. This pairing 
gives the brand a useful duality: `Manrope = engineered / contemporary` `Source 
Sans 3 = readable / service-oriented` A more distinctive alternate system would 
be: **Space Grotesk + Inter** Space Grotesk is available under the SIL Open 
Font License and has more visible personality; Inter is explicitly 
designed/distributed for interface use through its official project. 
citeturn18search31turn18search2 I would reserve that combination for a more 
product/SaaS-like Lakefront identity. The Manrope/Source Sans route better 
preserves some warmth.

    B2["G-wave micro mark"] B --> B3["Wave-band illustrations"] C --> C1["Wave 
    landscapes → campaigns / About"] C --> C2["Water texture → backgrounds / 
    quotes"] C --> C3["Aerial shore → homepage / case studies"] C --> 
    C4["Waterfront technology → services / testimonials"] D --> D1["Navy →
